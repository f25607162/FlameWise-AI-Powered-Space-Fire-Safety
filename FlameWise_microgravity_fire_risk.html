Project solution

The problem

NASA has spent decades burning things on purpose in orbit, and the results are scattered across papers, datasets and instrument logs. Engineers and mission planners can’t easily compare them or turn them into decisions. FlameWise is our attempt to close that gap.

How we approached it

  1. Collect. Pull experiment records and metadata from NASA’s Physical Sciences Informatics repository and related publications. For each one we keep the same few fields: material, oxygen level, pressure, airflow, spread rate, and whether the flame went out.
  2. Standardize. Different experiments report things differently, so everything goes into common units in one table. Missing values stay missing, because filling them in would mean inventing data.
  3. Add the AI layer. A language model retrieves the matching records first and answers only from them. It writes summaries, answers plain-English questions, and warns when the evidence is thin or contradictory.
  4. Rank in the open. The risk score mixes spread rate, how easily the material ignites, and how low the oxygen can drop before the flame dies. The weights are visible and adjustable, so nobody has to trust a black box.
  5. Show it. A dashboard with a ranking, a condition explorer, a comparison view and a question box, built for busy engineers.

Why it’s useful

It’s built around the questions people actually ask, like which fabric is safest at a given oxygen level, or how lowering airflow changes spread rate. Every answer shows its sources, so users can check instead of just trusting the AI.

Where the prototype stands

The ranking logic, charts, data import, export and AI answers all work. The numbers in the sample are placeholders, not NASA measurements. Real records can be loaded on the Your data page.

Limitations

  • Microgravity experiments are small, so results may not predict a large fire.
  • Ranking quality depends on how much data exists for each material.
  • The AI helps explore the data. It doesn’t replace expert review or physical testing.

Next steps

Load more datasets, validate the rankings with combustion researchers, add alerts for conditions near known flammability limits, and support offline use for deep-space missions.

How the score works

Risk is a weighted mix of three parts: spread rate under the current conditions (scaled against 2 cm/s), ignition ease, and how far below normal air the flame can still burn. If the flame goes out under the current conditions, the score is cut to 40%. Spread uses a simplified model: base spread times a square-root oxygen term times an airflow factor that peaks near 8 cm/s. Evidence runs do not change the score, but fewer than 6 runs earns a Thin tag. This ranks materials against each other and is not a physics simulation.
